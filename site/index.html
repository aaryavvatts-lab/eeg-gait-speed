Walking speedfrom the scalp

A machine learning project that looked like it worked at 92 percent, and what was left of it once I fixed the way it was tested.

EEG and leg muscle recordings from 58 people walking at three speeds, from a public PhysioNet dataset. A random forest on frequency band power, tested by holding out one person at a time.

The nine figures from the write-up, in order. Drag or scroll sideways.

Why the first number was wrong

Take EEG recorded while people walk at three speeds. Cut it into short windows, measure how much power sits in each frequency band, and train a model to name the speed. That gave me 0.923 balanced accuracy, which sounds great and is close to meaningless.

Each one minute recording gets cut into 58 windows that overlap by half. Split those windows at random and near copies land on both sides of the training and test sets, so the model scores well by recognising a window it has already mostly seen.

There is a second problem underneath that one. The same features can name which of 58 people a window came from 0.568 of the time, against a chance level of 1.7 percent, about 33 times better than guessing. That personal signature is strong enough to swamp the thing I was actually trying to measure. Splitting the data by person instead of by window is the only version that answers the question a real device would face, and that drops the score to 0.528.

Brain, muscle, and both

The dataset also carries muscle sensor recordings from the legs, which makes the honest comparison possible. Letting the model calibrate on a short recording from each person first:

Muscle beats brain by a wide margin. I also compared my results against a 2019 study (opens in a new tab) that used a two layer electrode to record movement artifact on its own, separately from brain activity, and found that most of what my brain model reads looks like body movement rather than the brain. The full write-up goes through exactly how I checked that.

With muscle in the mix the raw guesses are already right almost every second, so smoothing only adds delay, best left at zero. Brain signals alone are so jittery that the command needs about twelve seconds of averaging before it stops flipping between speeds, and by then it is reacting to what the rider wanted twelve seconds ago. For a chair, that settling time is the number that decides whether you could ride it.

What this does not show

Every person walked the three speeds in the same fixed order in one sitting, so speed is tangled up with anything that drifts over the course of a session, such as the electrodes settling in. That cannot be undone with this dataset. The calibrated numbers use the held out person's own recordings, so they describe a device with a short calibration step, not one that works cold. The group tested is 58 healthy adults on a treadmill at slow, fixed speeds, which is not the same as a wheelchair user walking at their own pace outdoors. And the people in this dataset were walking, so this says nothing about reading an intended speed from somebody who is sitting still, which is what an actual brain-computer interface for a wheelchair would need to do.
